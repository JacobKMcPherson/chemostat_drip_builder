Read the correction column before you report killing. Removing broth does not change CFU/mL — you take cells and medium in the same proportion. Replacing that volume with sterile diluent does. The correction is the log₁₀ you must add back to an observed count to recover the change attributable to drug:

Δlog₁₀ CFU (drug) = log₁₀(N_obs / N₀) − log₁₀(dilution factor)

The factor is (1 − ΔV/V)steps in exchange mode — flat, predictable, and small. In dilution mode it is V_peak/V(t), which compounds at exactly kₑ and is the reason that mode confounds kill measurement.

7 · Pharmacodynamic response

Where this exposure lands on the three PK/PD indices.

Loads illustrative Hill parameters and marks the index that class is normally driven by. Edit any field to switch to custom.
Untreated growth over the same window. The top of every curve.
Full range from control growth to maximum kill.
y axis: log₁₀ CFU reduction vs control fitted response stasis & EI₅₀ ◆ your measured result

Plotted as effect — killing attributable to drug, rising from zero to Emax — which is the conventional sigmoid Emax presentation. The dashed horizontal line marks stasis: below it the drug is not outrunning growth, above it you are clearing. Effect and the reported change in count are the same quantity viewed from opposite ends:

E(I) = E_max · IH / (EI₅₀H + IH) Δlog₁₀ CFU = E₀ − E(I)

The measured point uses your control arm where you have recorded one, falling back to the E₀ field otherwise — an observed reduction is only meaningful against something, and a measured control beats an assumed one.

One regimen cannot tell you which index is driving the effect. Within a single schedule the three indices move together — raise the dose and %T>MIC, AUC/MIC and Cmax/MIC all rise at once, so all three curves will “fit” equally well. Separating them is precisely what dose fractionation is for: hold the total daily dose constant and redistribute it across different numbers of doses per day. Only the driving index stays predictive when you do that. The marked point is where your current schedule sits; it is a prediction to test, not a result.

Indices are computed from the delivered staircase, not the ideal curve, by trapezoid over the interval endpoints — the same arithmetic you would apply to assayed samples. AUC is integrated over one full cycle (Phase A + Phase B) and scaled to 24 h on the assumption that the regimen repeats. Because the schedule ends mid-decay, that scaling is an extrapolation; shorten it by running more half‑lives.

The default Hill parameters are illustrative class-typical values, not calibrated constants — real EI₅₀ values shift with organism, strain, inoculum, growth phase, and endpoint window. Fit your own from your kill-curve data and type them in.

12 · Bench procedure

    

      
    
        
  1. Calibrate the drop factor once per tubing lot. Count 100 drops of your actual diluent into a tared tube and weigh it. Viscous or surfactant-containing media give drops well off the nominal 60 gtt/mL; using the number on the box is the largest avoidable error in this whole protocol.
    2. 
        
  2. Set the clamp, then count. Count drops for a full 60 s twice and average. Type that number into the rate field — do not type the rate you were aiming for.
    3. 
        
  3. Re-count at every interval. Gravity head falls as the reservoir empties, so the rate drifts down over a long run. If the count has moved more than ~10%, retype it and the remaining schedule recomputes.
    4. 
        
  4. Run Phase A by drop count. Open the clamp, count the listed drops, close it. Mix between steps — the model assumes the vessel is well mixed within each interval.
    5. 
        
  5. At peak, switch reservoirs from drug stock to plain diluent. Note the time; that is t = 0 for Phase B.
    6. 
        
  6. Run Phase B. In exchange mode, withdraw the listed volume first, then replace it by counting drops. Withdrawing first keeps the volume from transiently rising and is the natural place to take your timed sample.
    7. 
        
  7. Sample on the plan, record as you go. Enter each assay result in section 8 while the run is live — the correction column tells you immediately whether an apparent drop is drug or dilution.
    8. 
        
  8. Verify with assay, not arithmetic. Fit the observed k from your own concentrations and report that, not the intended one. Section 9 does the fit and shows you the gap.

13 · The math being used

    

      
With nothing leaving the vessel, concentration is mass over a moving volume, so both phases are volume bookkeeping.

      
q = n / D          mL/min from n drops/min at D gtt/mL

      
Phase A. All added drug stays, so cumulative added volume Q and concentration are locked together. With f = 1/R the target-to-stock ratio and u = 1 − e−kat:

      
C(t) = C_stock · Q / (V₀ + Q)        →        Q(t) = V₀ · f · u / (1 − f · u)

      
Each interval delivers the difference in Q. Because f is small, the required flow is very nearly V₀·f·ka·e−kat — it halves every absorption half‑time, which is the shape of the Phase A table.

      
Phase B, exchange. Remove and replace a fixed fraction each interval; volume is constant and concentration falls geometrically:

      
ΔV / V = 1 − e−kₑ·Δt

      
Phase B, dilution only. Mass is fixed, so C = M/V and exponential decay demands exponential volume growth — the vessel doubles every half‑life, and cells dilute at exactly kₑ, confounding your CFU counts with the clearance you are trying to impose:

      
V(t) = V_peak · e+kₑ·t        q(t) = kₑ · V(t)

      
Observed elimination is fitted by unweighted least squares on ln C against time, over the points after Cmax. AUC is linear trapezoid over the observed points, extrapolated to infinity by C_last/kₑ:

      
ln C = ln C₀ − kₑ·t        AUC∞ = AUC₀₋ₜ + C_last / kₑ

    

  


  

    Fed-batch approximation of one-compartment kinetics. Assumes complete mixing within each interval, no drug binding to vessel or tubing, and no degradation. Validate against assayed concentrations before treating the nominal half‑life as real.
